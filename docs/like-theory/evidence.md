# Evidence: what the research says, and how firmly

The October study (/d/bb57af4a) already covers the Big Five's structure, the reliability of proper instruments, and how small but real the life-outcome effects are. This note doesn't repeat any of that. It adds the four things the new theory leans on.

## 1. IPIP is public domain

**Firm.** The IPIP site says the pool "has been placed in the public domain", so permission "has already been automatically granted for any person to use IPIP items, scales, and inventories for any purpose, commercial or non-commercial". No need to ask. [IPIP permission page](https://ipip.ori.org/newPermission.htm)

One naming note from the same site: the "Mini-IPIP" (Donnellan et al. 2006, 20 items) is a short form of the 50-item *lexical* Big Five markers, not of the NEO-style set. Its "Neuroticism" is the reversed Emotional Stability scale, and its fifth factor is "Intellect or Imagination". [Mini-IPIP key](https://ipip.ori.org/MiniIPIPKey.htm). The 30-facet items quoted in the OCEAN map come from the [300-item facet key](https://ipip.ori.org/newNEOFacetsKey.htm) and Johnson's [IPIP-NEO-120](https://ipip.ori.org/30FacetNEO-PI-RItems.htm). Its alphas are .63 to .88 per facet, from an internet sample of 619,150.

## 2. Ranking limits comparing people, and the workaround

**Firm on the limit. The fix is real but conditional.**

- Ranking options against each other, scored the classical way, gives *ipsative* scores: they describe a person relative to their own profile. Brown and Maydeu-Olivares say this distorts profiles, validity and reliability when the scores are used to compare people, and that the cause is the scoring, not the format. They propose Thurstonian IRT, a model of the comparisons themselves, which can recover scores that can be compared. [Brown & Maydeu-Olivares 2013, *Psychological Methods* 18(1), 36–52, abstract via Kent repository](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/30530/)
- The fix has conditions. In simulations, measuring up to five traits with equally keyed blocks "does not yield sufficiently accurate trait scores … persons' trait scores remain partially ipsative and, thus, do not allow for valid comparisons between persons". With 30 traits it worked well. [Bürkner, Schulte & Holling 2019, *Educational and Psychological Measurement*, PDF opened, abstract read](https://www.uni-muenster.de/imperia/md/content/psychologie_institut_4/ae_holling/buerknerschulteholling_2019_limitations_of_tirt.pdf)

**What it means for us.** We have six leans, and every option carries hand-set weights on several of them, so our blocks aren't a clean Thurstonian design. Our centred scoring makes the leans partly trade off against each other. That's why the result reads best as *you against yourself*, and why comparing people should wait for **the room**, built from real players, rather than leaning on the score scale. A Thurstonian rescoring is possible later, but at six traits the paper above says don't expect it to fix person-to-person comparison on its own.

## 3. Scenario choices versus rating yourself

**Moderate on validity. Promising but thin on faking.**

- Validity. A meta-analysis of situational judgement tests found that "behavioral tendency" instructions, *what would you do*, which is our format, correlate more with personality, and "knowledge" instructions correlate more with cognitive ability. The instruction type made little difference to how well the tests predicted job performance, and SJTs added prediction "over cognitive ability, the Big 5, and over a composite" of both. [McDaniel, Hartman, Whetzel & Grubb 2007, *Personnel Psychology* 60, 63–91, PDF opened, abstract read](https://mcdaniel97.github.io/Publications/McDaniel,%20Hartman,%20Whetzel%20&%20Grubb%20(2007)%20pp%20SJT.pdf). That's employment testing, not personal reflection, so it carries over only loosely.
- Faking. In two lab studies, a construct-based SJT for conscientiousness was compared with the NEO-FFI: "the NEO-FFI was more susceptible to faking than the SJT". [Kasten, Freund & Staufenbiel 2020, *European Journal of Psychological Assessment* 36(1), 136–148, abstract only](https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/sweet-little-lies-an-in-depth-analysis-of-faking-behavior-on-situ/)
- **Where it's thin.** I found no meta-analysis that directly compares faking on SJTs with Likert self-ratings. The search turned up single studies and theses. Forced-choice formats, a cousin of ours, have mixed meta-analytic results on resisting faking. I saw that in search summaries only and didn't open the paper, so I've left it uncited.

So the honest claim stands as Watson put it: **harder to fool yourself, not objective.** It is still you saying what you'd do.

## 4. Can people move a lean on purpose?

**Moderate, and growing. The effects are small to medium, and mostly self-reported.**

- Wanting it helps a little. Students who wanted to change a trait changed more in that direction over 16 weeks. A vague "list three ways" prompt didn't help. Coaching people to form **if-then plans** did. The changes were "very modest, but statistically significant". [Hudson & Fraley 2015, *JPSP* 109, 490–507, via the BPS Research Digest summary](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/you-can-change-your-personality-will). I couldn't open the PubMed abstract because it was cookie-walled, so I didn't see the effect sizes.
- **Doing it is what counts.** Over 15 weeks people freely accepted weekly "challenges". "Merely accepting behavioral challenges did not predict trait changes. Rather, only actually completing challenges … predicted trait change over time." [Hudson, Briley, Chopik & Derringer 2018, *JPSP*, advance online, abstract read from the PDF](https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2018/11/Hudson-et-al.-2018.pdf). The Research Digest adds that accepting a challenge and then not doing it went with change in the *opposite* direction. It's observational, so cause isn't proven. [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/merely-desiring-alter-your-personality-not-enough-and-may-backfire-unless-you-take)
- **If-then plans work in general.** Across 94 tests, implementation intentions had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals, d = .65. [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38, 69–119, abstract](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f). Search summaries mention a much larger later meta-analysis with smaller pooled effects, but I didn't open it. Read d = .65 as the optimistic end.
- **The best trial so far.** PEACH, a three-month smartphone coaching app, was tested in a randomised trial of 1,523 people against a one-month waitlist. People who wanted more of a trait changed by d = 0.52, and people who wanted less by d = −0.58. Friends and partners saw the increases (d = 0.35) but not the decreases. Changes held three months after the end. [Stieger et al. 2021, *PNAS*, abstract via St Gallen repository](https://www.alexandria.unisg.ch/261917/)

**What it means for the loop.** The loop's design follows the evidence: ask what the person wants more or less of, turn it into a small if-then move in a named moment, and count the doing, not the signing up. The Hudson 2018 backfire is a design warning. A host that hands out tries people accept and then don't do may push them the wrong way, so fewer tries, done, beat many, accepted. **Thin spots:** almost all of this is self-report, most samples are students or volunteers who wanted to change, and decreases were invisible to observers in PEACH.

## 5. One more thing that bit while checking: collected answers and health data

Not asked for, but it constrains the collection design. The UK GDPR's special categories include "data concerning health" and "political opinions". [ICO, what is special category data](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-is-special-category-data/). Search summaries of the ICO's guidance on *inferences* say what counts is whether you *intend* to draw a health inference. I saw that in summaries only and didn't open the inference section. In practice: never infer anything health-like from a Sensitivity lean, and never ask IPIP's O6 Liberalism items, which ask how people vote.
