# What are you like, anyway? The theory, in one page

## What it does

You get everyday moments. Each has four things you might do. You put them in the order you'd actually do them, most like you first, and stop when none of the rest are like you. The moments are written so that every option is a reasonable thing to do. No option is the right answer.

So it isn't agree/disagree. You never rate yourself on a statement like "I am organised". You choose between concrete actions in a named situation, and every choice gives up one lean to buy another. That makes it much harder to flatter yourself. It's still you saying what you'd do, so the honest claim is **"harder to fool yourself"**, not "objective".

## What comes out

A **direction** on each of six leans, and how strongly you lean that way. A direction is never a judgment and never a type. Leaning towards "the familiar" is not a worse place to be than leaning towards "the unfamiliar". It's where you are.

Five of the leans share ground with the Big Five (OCEAN). The sixth, Risk appetite, is ours. The moments were written by hand and have never been checked against an OCEAN test, so the quiz is not an OCEAN measure. We can say which official OCEAN items each moment *shares ground with* (see the OCEAN map), and that's all we can say until we've measured it.

## What we can say, and what we can't

**We can say:**
- which way you lean on each line, and roughly how strongly;
- how far you sit from someone tapping at random (the middle, by construction);
- that more moments sharpen the picture ("10 is ok, more is better").

**We can't say yet:**
- where you'd stand **in the room**. Out of 100 people, how many are more X than you? That's the crown jewel, and with no players yet any number would be made up. The room comes from our own players: anonymous results build it, and placement then shows as a band ("around 35 to 55 of 100") that narrows as more people play and you answer more moments (see the collection design);
- that it predicts anything about your life.

The ranking format has one built-in limit. Each pick trades one lean against another, so the six leans partly add up to a fixed total. That makes the result strongest as **a picture of you against yourself**, which leans stand out for you. It's weaker as a comparison between two people. The room bands are how we earn the comparison honestly.

## The value: it ends in a move

Knowing your leans changes nothing on its own. The research is consistent: people who *want* to shift a trait shift it a little, but only when they actually do the small behaviours, not when they merely sign up for them. If-then plans tied to a named situation help (see evidence). So the result is the start of the useful part, not the end of it.

## The loop

1. **Result.** Your lean on each line.
2. **Want.** On each lean, do you want a bit more of it, a bit less, or is it fine as it is?
3. **Disagreements become things to try.** Where your pick in a moment points one way and your want points the other, that moment becomes the try. It's a real situation, in your own life, with the option you'd *like* to be picking: "Next time a friend texts 'pop in?' and the flat's a mess: 'Door's open!'"
4. **Plan it as if-then.** "If [the moment], then [the other option]." Before it happens, guess how it'll go. Afterwards, notice how it went.
5. **Replay.** Do the moments again in a few weeks. Your new lean against your old one shows the direction of travel. That's the only before-and-after that compares you with yourself, so it's the one the format is best at.

## Who carries it

The quiz gives the direction and the first move. Following it through over weeks needs a host: somewhere with a relationship, reminders and a memory of what you said you wanted. Cowch comes first. The engine stays embedded in hosts and is never a standalone product.
