# Reasonable Shape — movement discernment scout

*Dogma 3 applied to the movement field, 2026-08-20. Candidates only. Tom selects.*

---

## What this is

Four popular, results-getting treatments of the movement field, read for what they
**consistently** agree on — plus, separately and plainly, where they conflict or where the
data is misleading. Then 6–10 candidate weekly focus points built on the consistencies.

Nothing here is a decided programme. No domain structure is proposed. No coach copy is
written. This is the raw material a focus point is later made from.

**Defaults I took, flagged as asked:**

- **Four treatments**, because four covers the field cleanly without padding: one longevity
  clinician, one evolutionary scientist, one practitioner corpus, one guideline body. A fifth
  would have repeated one of them.
- **"Movement" includes everyday non-exercise activity** — walking, standing, stairs, floor
  time — alongside deliberate exercise. My call. The minimum-will register lives almost
  entirely down there.
- **Written for a sedentary-to-moderately-active adult in midlife starting near zero**, since
  that is the Reasonable Eating reader.

## Part 1 — the treatments

### 1. Outlive — Peter Attia (2023)

Longevity physician. #1 NYT bestseller, 3m+ copies, on the NYT list across 2023 and 2024.
Its exercise chapters are the most-quoted popular framing of movement-for-lifespan in
circulation: VO₂max and strength as the two biggest levers, and the "Centenarian Decathlon" —
train now for the physical tasks you want to still do at 90.

**Bias / blind spot:** critics — including cardiologist Eric Topol and several practising
physicians — argue Attia routinely runs ahead of outcome evidence, recommending protocols and
monitoring the trial data doesn't yet support. High biological plausibility, thin
randomised backing. Register is also high-effort: it assumes a reader who will train four-plus
hours a week.

### 2. Exercised — Daniel Lieberman (2020/21)

Harvard evolutionary biologist. Widely reviewed (NPR, Boston Globe), the standard
myth-busting treatment. Central claim: we never evolved to "exercise" — voluntary discretionary
physical activity is an unnatural act, so *not wanting to do it is normal, not a character
defect*. Humans evolved for moderate everyday activity and for staying active into old age.

**Bias / blind spot:** explanatory rather than prescriptive. It tells you why you don't want to
and why it matters; it is thin on what to do on a Tuesday. Evolutionary reasoning also runs
ahead of the direct evidence in places.

### 3. Built to Move — Kelly & Juliet Starrett (2023)

Physical therapist and co-author. NYT, Sunday Times and WSJ bestseller; the Starretts'
earlier books (*Becoming a Supple Leopard*, *Deskbound*) are the practitioner corpus behind it.
Ten "essential habits" framed as everyday physical vital signs — sit on the floor, get up
without hands, walk daily, breathe, sleep, balance on one leg. The closest existing book to
minimum-will register in this field.

**Bias / blind spot:** the mobility-and-mechanics worldview. Much of it is sensible and
low-risk; some of it (specific mobilisations, "tissue restoration") rests on practitioner
experience rather than trial evidence, and the book is not always clear which is which.

### 4. The guideline corpus — WHO 2020, and the US Physical Activity Guidelines 2nd ed. (2018)

Not a book, but by far the most-followed treatment of the field: the source every national
health service, GP and app quotes. 150–300 min/week moderate or 75–150 vigorous aerobic
activity; muscle-strengthening on 2+ days; reduce sedentary time. The 2018 US edition made
two changes that matter here — it dropped the old "must be in 10-minute bouts" rule, and it
led with *some is better than none*.

**Bias / blind spot:** built from observational cohort data, so its numbers are associations,
not proven causes. And the headline "150 minutes" is a population target that reads, to an
individual starting from zero, as a wall — which is the opposite of what its own evidence says.

## Part 2 — the consistencies

These survive across all four treatments, or all but one. Stated as claims a person could act on.

**1. The biggest returns are at the bottom of the dose curve.**
Going from nothing to a little buys more health than going from a lot to more. WHO/US
guidelines lead with it ("some is better than none"); Lieberman argues it from evolutionary
baseline; Attia agrees the move from the bottom quartile of fitness to the second is the single
largest mortality step. *Sources: guidelines, Lieberman, Attia.*

**2. Ordinary walking counts as real exercise.**
Step-count cohorts show mortality benefit beginning around 2,600 steps/day, with most of the
gain in by 6,000–8,000 in over-60s and 8,000–10,000 under 60. Not 10,000. Lieberman and the
Starretts both treat daily walking as the base layer; guidelines count it fully.
*Sources: guidelines, Lieberman, Starretts, step-count meta-analyses.*

**3. Strength work is not optional, and the effective dose is small.**
All four carry it. The dose is smaller than the culture implies: a 2022 meta-analysis
(Momma et al., 40+ cohorts) found any resistance training cut all-cause mortality ~15%, with
maximum benefit — about 27% — at roughly **30–60 minutes a week**, and benefit *diminishing*
beyond that. *Sources: all four.*

**4. Sitting is a separate problem from not exercising.**
You can hit the 150 minutes and still be harmed by eight unbroken sitting hours. WHO 2020 added
sedentary behaviour as its own recommendation. Breaking sitting up works: 2–5 minutes of light
walking, especially after a meal, cut post-meal glucose excursions ~17% versus staying seated.
*Sources: WHO, Starretts, Lieberman; Buffey et al. 2022.*

**5. Cardiorespiratory fitness is the strongest single modifiable predictor of dying.**
Mandsager et al. (JAMA Netw Open 2018, n=122,007) found elite-vs-low fitness carried ~80% lower
all-cause mortality — a larger spread than smoking, hypertension or diabetes in the same
dataset, and with no observed upper limit. Attia builds his case on this; guidelines and
Lieberman concur. *Sources: Attia primarily, guidelines and Lieberman supporting.*

**6. Short bursts of hard effort do real work.**
Stamatakis et al. (Nature Medicine 2022) tracked 25,241 self-described **non-exercisers**: three
daily bouts of 1–2 minutes of vigorous everyday movement — walking uphill, hurrying for a bus,
stairs — associated with 38–40% lower all-cause mortality and ~48% lower cardiovascular
mortality. This is the finding that makes intensity available to people who will never book a
session. *Sources: guidelines (2018 bout-rule removal), Lieberman; VILPA cohort.*

**7. Balance and getting off the floor are trainable, and they predict.**
Brito et al.: each one-point-lower score on the sitting-rising test carried ~21% higher
all-cause mortality. Balance-and-strength programmes (Otago, Tai Chi) reduce falls 20–58%
across 100+ studies. The Starretts make floor-sitting and one-leg standing explicit habits;
Attia's Centenarian Decathlon is the same idea in his language. *Sources: Starretts, Attia,
falls-prevention literature. Lieberman is quieter here.*

**8. The pattern is more forgiving than people think.**
Weekend-only activity carried a 32% lower all-cause mortality versus inactive — statistically
indistinguishable from spreading it across the week. The 10-minute-minimum bout rule was
dropped in 2018 because it had no evidential basis. *Sources: guidelines; weekend-warrior
cohorts (Circulation 2024, JAMA IM 2017).*

**9. Movement is a poor weight-loss lever and a superb everything-else lever.**
All four say some version of this: train for capacity, mood, blood sugar, sleep and lifespan;
eating is the weight lever. Lieberman and the constrained-energy literature are most explicit.
*Sources: all four. Boundary note: weight and appetite belong to the eating field, not this one.*

## Part 2b — where the data is misleading, or the sources conflict

Honest state of the evidence, not resolved by fiat.

**10,000 steps is not a finding.** It came from a 1965 Japanese pedometer name, *manpo-kei*
— "ten-thousand-step meter" — chosen because the character for 10,000 looks like a walking
person. The number entered global health advice as though it were research. Real dose curves
sit well below it. A reader who takes 10,000 as the bar and can't hit it concludes, wrongly,
that they've done nothing.

**Does exercise help you lose weight? Genuinely unsettled.** Pontzer's constrained-energy
model — built on the Hadza burning no more daily energy than sedentary Westerners — says the
body claws back most of what exercise spends. Intervention studies find compensation
averaging ~80% over 26 weeks; other researchers dispute both the magnitude and the mechanism.
**The magnitude is contested; the practical implication isn't.** Nobody in this literature
recommends exercise as a primary weight lever.

**Zone 2 is over-specified.** The popular claim — that there is a magic aerobic zone requiring
a heart-rate monitor — is far stronger than the evidence. A 2024 meta-analysis (17 RCTs, 437
subjects) found polarised training ahead on VO₂peak by a small-to-moderate margin, no more.
Most people who think they're training in zone 2 aren't. What is agreed: separate your easy
from your hard. That is the whole actionable content.

**Stretching does roughly none of what it's sold for.** Moderate-to-strong evidence says
routine static stretching does not reduce overall injury rates, and stretching immediately
before hard effort temporarily *lowers* force output. Meta-analysis of ~400 participants found
strength training improves range of motion about as well as stretching does. Flexibility as a
standalone goal is the weakest-supported thing in the popular canon.

**Almost all the mortality numbers above are observational.** Step counts, resistance-training
dose, VILPA, weekend warriors, the sitting-rising test — cohort studies, not trials. Healthy
people move more *because* they are healthy (reverse causation), and people who adhere to any
health advice do better at everything (the healthy-adherer effect). The direction of these
findings is consistent enough to act on; the precise percentages are not to be trusted.

**Two curves point opposite ways and both are quoted as fact.** Fitness-vs-mortality shows *no
upper limit* of benefit; step-count-vs-mortality *plateaus* around 8,000. Both are true of what
they measure. Quoting either as "the" dose-response is where popular advice goes wrong.

## Part 4 — candidate weekly focus points

Six to ten candidates, strongest first. Working names only — not Tom's voice, not final copy.
Each is an object of attention for a week, not a training prescription.

### 1. `Walk after / you eat`

**The week:** after your evening meal, go outside and walk for a few minutes. Two minutes
counts.
**Rests on:** consistency 4 (breaking up sitting) — Buffey et al. 2022, WHO 2020, Starretts.
**Bannister:** post-meal walking is an ordinary, centuries-old habit across southern Europe and
much of Asia. Millions do it daily without calling it exercise.
**Brother-in-law:** "I go for a little walk after dinner." He cannot make that funny.
**Weak spot:** it is the strongest candidate on evidence-per-unit-effort, but its headline
mechanism is blood glucose — which sits closer to eating than to movement. **Could plausibly
live in an EATING domain instead.**

### 2. `Take the / stairs`

**The week:** when there are stairs, take them, and let yourself get slightly out of breath.
**Rests on:** consistency 6 (short bursts of hard effort) — Stamatakis 2022 VILPA cohort;
2018 guidelines dropping the 10-minute bout rule.
**Bannister:** the VILPA cohort was 25,241 people who described themselves as non-exercisers.
The behaviour was measured in ordinary lives, not prescribed to athletes.
**Brother-in-law:** "I take the stairs." Nothing to laugh at.
**Weak spot:** unavailable to anyone whose week contains no stairs, and to some with knee or hip
trouble. Needs a stated substitute (a hill, a brisk hundred yards).

### 3. `Get up / off the floor`

**The week:** sit on the floor once a day — to read, to watch something — and notice how you get
back up.
**Rests on:** consistency 7 (balance and floor capacity) — Brito sitting-rising test;
Starretts' habit; Attia's Centenarian Decathlon.
**Bannister:** the majority of the world's population sits on the floor daily as a matter of
course. This is a normal human posture we opted out of, not a skill to acquire.
**Brother-in-law:** "I sit on the floor in the evenings." Odd, maybe. Not laughable.
**Weak spot:** the sitting-rising evidence is one observational cohort, heavily
press-amplified. And for some midlife bodies getting down is genuinely hard — the honest
version needs a way in that doesn't start with failure.

### 4. `Some is / plenty`

**The week:** whenever you'd normally skip it because you haven't time to do it properly, do a
fraction instead. Five minutes counts as done.
**Rests on:** consistencies 1 and 8 (bottom of the dose curve; forgiving patterns) — 2018 US
guidelines, weekend-warrior cohorts, step-count curves.
**Bannister:** this is the behaviour of most people who stay active for decades; it is the
all-or-nothing pattern that is rare and fragile.
**Brother-in-law:** "I did five minutes." He *will* try to laugh at this one — and the answer,
that a 32% mortality gap sits between nothing and a little, is the sturdiest number in the
document.
**Weak spot:** it is a disposition more than an action, which makes it harder to notice you're
doing. Closest in spirit to the whole programme, and possibly too meta to be one week of it.

### 5. `Carry it / yourself`

**The week:** carry your own load — shopping, bags, the heavy thing across the room — instead of
finding the way round it.
**Rests on:** consistency 3 (strength, small effective dose) — Momma et al. 2022; all four
treatments.
**Bannister:** carrying is what human strength is *for*, and people who don't train do it
constantly. Grip and carry capacity track the same outcomes gym strength does.
**Brother-in-law:** "I carry my own shopping." Unmockable.
**Weak spot:** the honest translation from "resistance training 30–60 min/week" to "carry
things" is a stretch. Real load progression isn't in it. This is the best minimum-will
*pointer* at strength I can find — but say plainly that it points, and doesn't arrive.

### 6. `Stand up / every hour`

**The week:** when you notice you've been sitting a long stretch, stand up and move for a
minute. Not a timer — a noticing.
**Rests on:** consistency 4 (sedentary time as its own risk) — WHO 2020's new recommendation;
Starretts' *Deskbound*.
**Bannister:** anyone with a job that involves standing does far more than this without
thinking about it.
**Brother-in-law:** "I get up and stretch my legs." Invisible to mockery.
**Weak spot:** WHO explicitly could not quantify a sedentary threshold — the evidence supports
"less" but not any number. Overlaps candidate 1 mechanically.

### 7. `Stand on / one leg`

**The week:** while the kettle boils or you brush your teeth, stand on one leg.
**Rests on:** consistency 7 (balance) — falls-prevention literature (Otago, Tai Chi: 20–58%
fall reduction); Starretts' habit; the 10-second one-leg-stance marker.
**Bannister:** trivially demonstrated. It requires no equipment, space, clothing or time.
**Brother-in-law:** he can see it happening in a kitchen and has nothing to say about it.
**Weak spot:** the strong falls evidence is in *over-65s* and in structured multi-week
programmes. Extrapolating to a midlife adult standing on one leg by the kettle is plausible
and low-cost, but it is extrapolation.

### 8. `Get off / one stop early`

**The week:** build a walk into a journey you were making anyway — park further off, get off a
stop early, walk the errand.
**Rests on:** consistency 2 (walking counts; benefit from ~2,600 steps) — step-count
meta-analyses, Lieberman, guidelines.
**Bannister:** this is simply how people without cars live, and how most of the world moves.
**Brother-in-law:** "I walk the last bit." Fine.
**Weak spot:** heavily circumstance-dependent — a rural life with no bus and no pavement can't
run it. Weakest on universality, not on evidence.

### 9. `Puff once / a day`

**The week:** once a day, move hard enough that talking gets difficult — for a minute.
**Rests on:** consistencies 5 and 6 (cardiorespiratory fitness; short vigorous bursts) —
Mandsager 2018, Stamatakis 2022.
**Bannister:** the VILPA cohort again — non-exercisers, doing this incidentally, getting most
of the benefit.
**Brother-in-law:** "I get out of puff once a day." Says nothing about your ambitions, so gives
him nothing.
**Weak spot:** overlaps candidate 2 almost entirely — stairs are the concrete version of this
abstract one. Pick one, not both. **The breath-noticing element brushes a BREATHING domain;
noted and left there.**

### 10. `Move with / someone`

**The week:** do one piece of your movement with another person — a walk with a friend, with a
partner, on the phone while walking.
**Rests on:** adherence rather than a physiological consistency. Every treatment concedes the
programme you keep beats the programme that's optimal; Lieberman's whole argument is that
solo discretionary exercise is the unnatural bit and social activity is the natural one.
**Bannister:** walking groups, dog walks and walk-and-talks are ubiquitous.
**Brother-in-law:** "I go for a walk with a mate." Below the threshold of comment.
**Weak spot:** the honesty note is the biggest one in this list — this rests on Lieberman's
argument and general adherence research, **not** on a movement consistency from Part 2. It is
here because it is the candidate most likely to make the other nine survive, not because the
evidence for it is strong.

## What I'd flag to Tom

Three things, and none of them are decisions I should make.

**The strength gap is real and I could not close it.** The evidence for resistance training is
as solid as anything in this field, and the honest minimum-will translation of it — `Carry it
yourself` — points at strength without arriving there. Every version I tried that actually
delivers progressive load ("two sets of press-ups twice a week") fails the register: it is a
training prescription, not an object of attention. That gap is a finding, not an omission.

**Two candidates are load-bearing duplicates.** `Take the stairs` and `Puff once a day` are the
same physiological claim at different altitudes; `Walk after you eat` and `Stand up every hour`
are the same sedentary-interruption claim. Ten candidates is really about seven distinct ideas.

**Your own movement principles aren't anywhere I can reach.** A full sweep of the estate —
including all 8,115 conversation transcripts — found you *saying* you have them and nowhere
saying what they are. Part 3 has the detail. If you dictate them, they should probably outrank
anything in Part 4.

**One candidate belongs to another scout.** `Walk after you eat` is the strongest thing in the
document on evidence-per-unit-effort, and its mechanism is glucose. If eating claims it,
movement loses its best card — worth knowing before the selection pass.
