# Reasonable Shape — discernment: sleep and breathing

*Scout report to Tom, 2026-08-20. Dogma 3 executed on two fields: what the most-read
evidence-based authorities agree on, where they conflict, where the data misleads, and
6–10 candidate focus points per field. Selection is yours — that is the only-Tom part.*

**The short answer to the structural question, up front: breathing does not earn its own
domain. Three domains — EAT, MOVE, SLEEP — with four focus points each. Breath rides in as
one focus point inside SLEEP and one inside MOVE, where the good breathing wisdom actually
lives.** Reasoning in Part 3.

---

## Part 1 — SLEEP

### The treatments

**Matthew Walker, *Why We Sleep* (2017).**
The book everyone has heard of. International bestseller, the reason "sleep" became a
mainstream health topic at all. On the list for reach and influence, **not** for reliability —
see the conflicts section, which is substantial.

**Colin Espie, *Overcoming Insomnia and Sleep Problems* (2006, revised 2nd ed.).**
The British clinical standard. Espie is Professor of Sleep Medicine at Oxford and
co-founder of Sleepio, the digital CBT-I programme validated across sixteen pre-registered
RCTs and given a NICE guideline in May 2022. On the list for clinical standing — this is
the closest thing the field has to a peer-reviewed self-help book.

**Jade Wu, *Hello Sleep* (2023).**
Behavioural sleep medicine specialist. The current best-selling popular CBT-I book, and the
one that most directly targets the *anxiety about sleep* rather than the sleep. On the list
for being the modern popular entry point that is also clinically sound.

**Russell Foster, *Life Time* (2022).**
Circadian neuroscientist, Oxford, FRS. Sunday Times bestseller in hardback and paperback.
On the list because it carries the body-clock half of the field, which the insomnia books
mostly assume rather than explain.

**The clinical guidelines, as a fifth "treatment".**
Not a book, but it is what the field actually agrees. The American College of Physicians
(2016) recommends CBT-I as the **initial** treatment for all adults with chronic insomnia,
before any medication. Every book above is downstream of this.

### What they consistently agree on

1. **Regular timing beats duration.** A fixed wake time, seven days a week, is the single
   highest-leverage thing an ordinary person can do. *Espie, Wu, Foster, and the guidelines,
   all four.* Backed hard outside the books: in 60,977 UK Biobank participants, sleep
   *regularity* predicted all-cause mortality more strongly than sleep *duration* —
   20–48% lower risk in the most regular.

2. **Morning light sets the clock; evening light delays it.** Get outdoor light early, dim
   things down late. *Foster carries this in depth; Walker, Wu and Espie all carry it.*

3. **The bed is a cue, not a place to lie awake.** Stimulus control — bed for sleep only, and
   if you are awake and cross, get up — is the most evidence-supported behavioural component
   in the whole field. *Espie, Wu, guidelines. It is a named core component of CBT-I.*

4. **Trying harder makes it worse.** Sleep effort is self-defeating; the anxiety about not
   sleeping is a larger part of chronic insomnia than the sleep loss. *Wu builds her whole
   book on this; Espie carries it as cognitive therapy.* This one maps exactly onto the
   no-civil-war doctrine.

5. **Caffeine and alcohol are dose-and-timing problems, not moral ones.** *All four.* A 2023
   meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews put a number on it: a standard coffee needs
   roughly **8.8 hours** before bed to avoid cutting total sleep time; a pre-workout dose
   needs 13.2.

6. **Cool, dark, quiet, and a wind-down buffer.** Universal across all four — and see the
   caveat below: this is the *weakest*-evidenced consistency in the list, not the strongest.

7. **Snoring plus daytime sleepiness is a medical question, not a habits question.** *Espie,
   Wu, Walker, Foster.* No behavioural focus point fixes sleep apnoea.

### Where they conflict, and where the data misleads

**The big one: *Why We Sleep* has serious, documented factual problems.** In 2019 Alexey
Guzey published a detailed critique alleging the book is riddled with scientific and factual
errors, having spent ~130 hours fact-checking Chapter 1 alone. The specific challenges that
matter for us:

- *"The shorter your sleep, the shorter your lifespan."* The actual meta-analytic picture is
  a **U-shape** — both short and long sleep track higher mortality, with around 7 hours
  sitting at the bottom. The book presents half a curve.
- *"Sleeping under 6–7 hours more than doubles your risk of cancer."* A 2018 systematic
  review across ~1.5 million participants found no association between sleep duration and
  cancer risk in either direction.
- *"The WHO has declared a sleep loss epidemic."* No such declaration has been found.
- *"Two-thirds of adults fail to get the recommended eight hours."* The recommendation is a
  **range**, 7–9 hours, not a fixed eight.

UC Berkeley reviewed the matter and characterised the problems as "minor". Walker published
a blog response but stated it addressed reader questions rather than Guzey's specific
criticisms. **Popularity is not evidence.** Treat the book as a field-opener, and take no
number from it.

**What survives the criticism.** Quite a lot, and I am not over-correcting into "nobody knows
anything". Sleep matters. Circadian regularity is robustly evidenced. CBT-I outperforms
sleeping pills for chronic insomnia and is the guideline first-line treatment. Short sleep is
genuinely associated with worse cardiometabolic outcomes. What does *not* survive is the
threat register — the "you are killing yourself" framing that a good chunk of the popular
field runs on. For our purposes that framing is doubly disqualified: it is over-stated,
**and** it is precisely the internal civil war we refuse to start.

**Sleep hygiene alone is close to useless as a treatment.** The AASM recommends hygiene rules
for everyone but finds insufficient evidence for hygiene as *monotherapy* — it is adjunctive
only. A 2025 systematic review found sleep hygiene education inferior to CBT-I, partial
CBT-I, exercise and even acupressure. Honest implication for us: the "cool dark room, no
screens" material everyone repeats is the *weakest* thing in the field, and the behavioural
components — fixed wake time, stimulus control, dropping sleep effort — are the strong ones.
If we build a sleep domain out of hygiene tips we will have built the part that does not work.

**Blue-light glasses do not have the evidence their marketing implies.** A Cochrane review of
17 RCTs concluded blue-light-filtering spectacles probably make no difference to sleep
quality; the sleep evidence specifically was rated very low certainty, with three trials
positive and three null. Dimming the room is a different and better-supported claim than
buying orange glasses.

**Sleep trackers can make it worse.** "Orthosomnia" — anxiety driven by chasing a good sleep
score — is a documented clinical pattern, though **not** a formal diagnosis and not
established by RCT. Flagging honestly: the mechanism is well-described and clinicians report
it, the evidence base is qualitative and observational.

**Explicit gap.** I did not verify a specific meta-analysis on alcohol and sleep architecture
in this pass. The four treatments agree alcohol fragments the second half of the night, and I
am reporting that as book consensus rather than as a study I checked. If a focus point is
built on it, verify first.

### Candidate focus points — SLEEP

**1. Same wake time**
Get up at the same time every day, weekends included, regardless of how the night went.
*Evidence:* the strongest single item in the field — Espie, Wu, Foster, guidelines, plus the
UK Biobank regularity finding.
*Bannister:* every person who has ever held a job has done this.
*Brother-in-law:* "I get up at the same time each day." Unremarkable. Passes easily.

**2. Get light early**
Ten to twenty minutes of outdoor light within an hour or so of waking.
*Evidence:* Foster's core mechanism; carried by all four.
*Bannister:* dog walkers do it daily without calling it a protocol.
*Brother-in-law:* "I go outside in the morning." Passes.

**3. Dim the last hour**
Turn the lights down in the last hour, rather than banning screens.
*Evidence:* light-timing consensus is solid; the specific screen-and-glasses layer is not
(Cochrane). This candidate is deliberately written to sit on the strong claim.
*Bannister:* trivially done.
*Brother-in-law:* passes. "Bright lights late keep me up" is a normal sentence.

**4. Last coffee by lunch**
Final caffeine of the day around midday.
*Evidence:* the 2023 Sleep Medicine Reviews meta-analysis — ~8.8 hours for a standard coffee.
*Bannister:* millions of people do exactly this.
*Brother-in-law:* passes, though heavy coffee drinkers will push back. That is a fit
question, not a register question.

**5. Bed is for sleeping**
If you are lying there awake and annoyed, get up and go elsewhere until you are sleepy.
*Evidence:* stimulus control — a core CBT-I component, Espie and Wu, first-line in guidelines.
*Bannister:* yes, and it is what clinicians instruct.
*Brother-in-law:* passes. It sounds like common sense because it is.

**6. Don't chase the eight**
Stop targeting a number. Judge the night by how the day feels.
*Evidence:* the recommendation is a 7–9 range; the mortality relationship is U-shaped; and
sleep effort is itself a cause of insomnia (Wu).
*Bannister:* it is the removal of a behaviour, so trivially possible.
*Brother-in-law:* passes, and it is the most no-civil-war item in the whole list.

**7. Take the tracker off**
A week with no sleep score. Notice how you feel instead.
*Evidence:* orthosomnia — well-described clinically, **weak evidence base** (qualitative and
observational, no RCT). **Flagged as borderline on evidence.** Doctrinally it is one of the
strongest things here; empirically it is the softest. Your call.
*Bannister:* yes.
*Brother-in-law:* passes, mildly. "I've stopped wearing the ring at night" is fine.

**8. Wind down, don't power down**
A twenty-minute buffer between the last task and the pillow. Not a ritual, a gap.
*Evidence:* book consensus across all four; part of the weaker hygiene cluster, so honestly
graded as sensible rather than proven.
*Bannister:* yes.
*Brother-in-law:* passes.

**9. Drink earlier, not later**
If you are drinking, finish well before bed.
*Evidence:* book consensus. **Explicit gap — I did not verify a primary source this pass.**
*Bannister:* yes.
*Brother-in-law:* passes, and it is a moderation-free framing — it moves the clock, not
the amount, which keeps it out of the civil war.

**10. Get the snore checked**
If you snore and you are sleepy in the day, book the GP appointment.
*Evidence:* unanimous across all four; apnoea is common and badly under-diagnosed.
*Bannister:* yes.
*Brother-in-law:* passes.
**Flagged as borderline on register:** this is a task, not an attention practice. It costs an
appointment and a wait, which sits outside minimum will. It may belong in the hub material or
week 13 rather than as a weekly focus point — but it is too important to drop silently.

## Part 2 — BREATHING

### The treatments

**James Nestor, *Breath: The New Science of a Lost Art* (2020).**
Over two million copies, 18 weeks on the New York Times list, shortlisted for the 2021 Royal
Society Science Book Prize. This is the field's *Why We Sleep* — in both senses. On the list
for reach.

**Patrick McKeown, *The Oxygen Advantage* (2015).**
The Buteyko lineage made athletic. In sixteen languages; the standard reference for
nose-breathing and CO₂-tolerance training. On the list for influence — most of what
circulates online about breathing traces back here.

**Wim Hof, *The Wim Hof Method* (2020), and the associated trials.**
Cyclic hyperventilation plus cold. On the list because it is the highest-profile breathing
protocol in the world **and** because it has actual published trials, which is rare here.

**The clinical/experimental literature, as a fourth "treatment".**
Not a book, and I am including it deliberately because in this field the books are weaker
than the papers. Three strands matter: Balban et al. 2023 in *Cell Reports Medicine* on
brief structured breathing; the slow-breathing and blood-pressure literature; and the
Buteyko-for-asthma trials.

### What they consistently agree on

1. **Slow the breath down and arousal comes down with it.** *All three books, plus the
   papers.* This is the one genuinely robust, mechanistically-understood finding in the field.

2. **The out-breath is the lever.** Longer exhale than inhale calms; the pattern is
   parasympathetic. *Nestor, McKeown, and the strongest single study in the field:* Balban et
   al. 2023 — a randomised controlled trial of three 5-minute daily breathwork practices
   against mindfulness meditation, in which **exhale-emphasised cyclic sighing produced the
   greatest mood improvement and the greatest drop in respiratory rate**.

3. **Around six breaths a minute is a real physiological resonance, not a mystical number.**
   *McKeown and the physiology literature.* It matches the frequency of Mayer waves and
   synchronises respiration, heart rate and blood pressure. A 2012 meta-analysis of
   device-guided slow breathing across 8 studies and 494 adults found systolic −3.67 mmHg and
   diastolic −2.51 mmHg; the American Heart Association's 2013 statement rated device-guided
   breathing Class IIa, level B.

4. **Nose, not mouth, at rest.** *All three books, emphatically.* The mechanism — nasal nitric
   oxide reaching the lungs — is real and measured. The size of the everyday benefit is the
   contested part.

5. **Breathing is trainable and it is free.** *All three.* No equipment, no time cost, no
   purchase. In minimum-will terms this field is unusually well-behaved.

6. **Habitual upper-chest breathing is a real pattern with real symptoms.** *All three, plus
   the dysfunctional-breathing RCT literature* — breathing retraining improves autonomic
   function and symptoms in people who have that pattern. Note the qualifier carefully: *in
   people who have that pattern.*

### Where they conflict, and where the data misleads

This half needs more care than the sleep half. The good physiology and the commercial
mythology are shelved side by side.

**Nestor's *Breath* overstates.** Reviewers — including a psychiatrist writing in The
Spectator — found it leans on anecdote and dated sources, cites unpublished studies, and
overstates the health benefits attributed to nasal nitric oxide and raised CO₂. It carries
claims with no evidential support at all, such as right-nostril breathing improving
digestion. One reviewer's summary — "a lot of woo and anecdote, with a little bit of science
hidden in the endnotes" — is harsh but not unfair. The famous Stanford nasal-obstruction
self-experiment at the heart of the book is n=2.

**Mouth taping does not have the evidence, and it has a real risk.** A 2025 systematic review
in PLOS One covering 10 studies and 233 patients found limited clinical benefit and genuine
safety concerns. **Every included study was rated poor quality.** The small positive signals
came only from mild obstructive sleep apnoea, in studies that had specifically excluded
people with nasal obstruction. For anyone with moderate or severe apnoea, removing the mouth
as a backup airway can worsen oxygen drops. The review explicitly does not recommend it.
This is the most-shared breathing intervention on the internet and it should not go anywhere
near a Reasonable Shape focus point.

**The Buteyko theory is not proven by the Buteyko results.** The trials are reasonably
consistent that Buteyko improves asthma symptoms and cuts reliever inhaler use — one trial
showed an 85% reduction at six months. But **lung function and bronchial responsiveness do
not change.** So something real is happening, and it is very unlikely to be the "chronic
over-breathing" mechanism the method claims. The honest reading: breathing retraining changes
symptom perception, anxiety and breathing pattern, and that is worth having — but do not ship
the CO₂-deficiency theory as fact.

**Wim Hof: weak evidence, non-trivial danger.** A 2023 systematic review found the studies
very low quality with high risk of bias throughout — small samples, no published protocols,
no blinding. There is one genuinely interesting finding (an adrenaline-mediated
anti-inflammatory response), and mixed results everywhere else. The danger is not
theoretical: cyclic hyperventilation induces hypocapnia, which **suppresses the urge to
breathe**, and there have been multiple drowning deaths, including a lawsuit filed in
December 2022 over the death of a 17-year-old. Never in or near water. This is a hard
exclusion for us on the brother-in-law test alone, before we even reach the evidence.

**Nose-breathing during hard exercise probably does not improve performance.** A recent
randomised cross-over trial in well-trained endurance athletes found no significant
differences between nasal, oronasal and oral breathing on VO₂max, minute ventilation,
respiratory frequency or peak power. Nasal breathing at easy effort is still defensible — as
an intensity governor and a habit-builder — but not on a performance claim.

**Explicit gap.** I did not find, in this pass, a large randomised trial of *slow breathing
practice as a daily habit* on hard outcomes in healthy people. The blood-pressure evidence is
mostly in hypertensives and often device-guided; the mood evidence is Balban's one-month RCT.
The field's honest position is "reliable short-term physiological effect, thin long-term
outcome evidence" — and I would rather report that than launder it.

### Candidate focus points — BREATHING

**1. Breathe out longer**
Make the out-breath longer than the in-breath, a few times a day.
*Evidence:* the single best-supported item in the field — Balban 2023 RCT, plus all three
books.
*Bannister:* everyone already does this involuntarily when they sigh.
*Brother-in-law:* passes. "I breathe out slowly when I'm wound up." Nobody laughs.

**2. Five minutes of sighing**
Five minutes a day of cyclic sighing: two inhales through the nose, one long exhale.
*Evidence:* Balban 2023 — beat mindfulness meditation on mood and respiratory rate at five
minutes a day over a month.
*Bannister:* it was the trial protocol; ordinary participants sustained it.
*Brother-in-law:* borderline and **flagged**. "I do five minutes of cyclic sighing" is one
notch into protocol territory. The naming rescues it — "five minutes of breathing out" is
sayable where "cyclic sighing practice" is not.

**3. Six breaths a minute**
Slow to roughly six breaths a minute for a few minutes — five in, five out.
*Evidence:* Mayer-wave resonance, baroreflex, the BP meta-analysis, AHA Class IIa/B.
*Bannister:* it is the protocol used in clinical trials with ordinary patients.
*Brother-in-law:* passes if framed as "I slow my breathing down", fails if framed as
"resonance frequency breathing". Naming is doing the work again.

**4. Nose all day**
Breathe through your nose when you are not exerting yourself. Notice when you have drifted to
mouth.
*Evidence:* unanimous across the books; nasal NO is real; **the magnitude of the everyday
benefit is over-claimed** — say so.
*Bannister:* it is most people's default already.
*Brother-in-law:* passes.

**5. Nose while you walk**
Keep it nasal at easy effort — walking, easy cycling. If you can't, you are going too hard.
*Evidence:* honest framing is as a **pace governor**, not a performance aid; the performance
claim did not survive the randomised trial.
*Bannister:* yes.
*Brother-in-law:* passes.
**Flagged: this is a MOVE focus point wearing a breathing hat.** It matters for Part 3.

**6. One breath before you answer**
One slow out-breath before you reply — to a message, a question, a provocation.
*Evidence:* extrapolated from the arousal-reduction literature rather than tested directly.
**Flagged: weakest evidence in the list.** Strong on doctrine — it is pure noticing, which is
exactly minimum will — thin on data.
*Bannister:* yes.
*Brother-in-law:* passes.

**7. Breathe low**
Breathe into the belly rather than the upper chest.
*Evidence:* dysfunctional-breathing retraining RCTs show real improvement — **in people who
have the pattern**. "Everyone breathes wrong" is a sales line, not a finding.
*Bannister:* yes.
*Brother-in-law:* passes.

**8. Ten breaths at lights out**
Ten slow breaths, long exhales, once you are in bed.
*Evidence:* the arousal-reduction evidence, applied at sleep onset.
*Bannister:* yes.
*Brother-in-law:* passes.
**Flagged: this is a SLEEP focus point wearing a breathing hat.** Also relevant to Part 3.

**Explicitly excluded, and why:** mouth taping (poor-quality evidence, real risk with
undiagnosed apnoea); Wim Hof cyclic hyperventilation and any breath-hold work (weak evidence,
deaths on record, fails the brother-in-law test outright); CO₂-tolerance breath-hold tables
(rests on a mechanism the trials do not support).

## Part 3 — THE STRUCTURAL ANSWER

**Does breathing carry enough independent, consistent, focus-point-shaped wisdom to stand as
its own domain?** No. Run it against the three tests.

### Test 1 — enough distinct wisdom to fill three or four focus points?

**Marginal pass, and only just.** There are exactly two genuinely independent ideas in the
whole field: *slow down, with a longer out-breath* and *use your nose*. Everything else on
my candidate list is one of those two applied in a different context — at your desk, in bed,
on a walk, before you reply. Three or four focus points built on two ideas would repeat
themselves. Compare the eating domain, where "chew until liquid", "hunger >> appetite" and
"did it grow into its shape?" are three unrelated pieces of knowledge.

### Test 2 — are the good ones independent of movement and sleep?

**Clear fail, and this is the decisive one.** Look at what happened to my own list without me
steering it. "Nose while you walk" is a MOVE focus point. "Ten breaths at lights out" is a
SLEEP focus point. Those are the two most concrete, most obviously useful breathing items I
found, and both of them are about *breath during another activity*. The remaining ones — the
sighing, the six-a-minute — are not really about shape at all; they are stress management
that happens to run through the lungs. Honest verdict: the good breathing focus points are
breath-during-exercise and breath-for-sleep-onset wearing a different hat, exactly as the
brief suspected.

### Test 3 — does an ordinary person grok BREATHE as one of the things that gets them in shape?

**Fail.** Eat, move, sleep is a sentence a person already believes before you say it. Eat,
move, sleep, breathe reads as three obvious things and one odd one — and worse, it reads as
*the sort of thing a wellness person says*, which is the exact register Reasonable Shape has
to stay out of. Dogma 4 asks for divisions that are easy to grok. Three passes; four does not.

### The recommendation

**Three domains — EAT, MOVE, SLEEP — with four focus points each, and breath enters as one
focus point inside SLEEP and one inside MOVE.**

Better: the breathing wisdom that survives discernment gets *used*, at the moment it actually
helps, instead of being padded out to fill a domain it cannot fill. Simpler: three words
everyone already believes, no explaining required, and the 3×4 symmetry you asked for lands
exactly on twelve plus the week-13 review. Cheaper in total: no fourth domain to research,
name, write coach banks for and maintain — and no ongoing cost of defending the odd wheel
every time someone asks why breathing is in there.

**The fork that genuinely remains**, and it is yours: if you *do* want four domains, the
honest fourth is not BREATHE. It is something in the shape of **CALM** or **RECOVER** —
down-regulation as a domain in its own right, of which breath is the main lever but not the
only one. That grades better on test 3 than BREATHE does, because ordinary people do accept
that being permanently wound up keeps them out of shape. I am not recommending it, because
three domains is simpler and eat-move-sleep is unimprovable as a sentence — but it is a real
option and not a consolation prize. Worth ten minutes of conversation rather than a decision
made off this document.

### The resulting shape

- **EAT** — four focus points, drawn from Reasonable Eating's existing twelve.
- **MOVE** — four focus points, from the sibling scout, one of which is breath-at-easy-effort.
- **SLEEP** — four focus points, one of which is breath-at-lights-out.
- **Week 13** — the review week, as in the flagship.

One honest cost to name plainly: **this compresses Reasonable Eating's twelve focus points
down to four.** Your own words — eating is "the biggest shortcut" and "the University of the
Will" — argue it deserves more than a third of the year. Two ways out, both yours to rule on:
either accept that Shape's EAT domain is a *four-point selection* and Reasonable Eating stays
the standalone deep programme for anyone who wants the full twelve; or keep eating's internal
HOW / WHEN / WHAT split and let it carry a domain-and-a-half. The first is cleaner. Either
way, choosing which four of the twelve survive is a discernment pass of its own, and it is
not one a scout should do for you.

## Part 4 — THE HUB, tentatively

*Raising the question, not answering it. This is a taste call and it is yours alone.*

Reasonable Eating's hub inverts the field's dominant script — restriction — with `EAT MORE`,
and the civil war is over before week one. Shape's dominant script is **fanaticism**: shape as
punishment, earned through suffering, deserved only after you have paid. The hub has to
dissolve *earning*.

**MORE LIFE** — the abundance move again, one level up. What you get is more of everything,
not less of anything. Sits naturally over all three domains.

**SHAPE HAPPENS** — shape as a by-product of ordinary days, not a project you undertake.
Kills the gym-punishment frame directly.

**STOP EARNING** — the most precise inversion of the actual script, and the riskiest: it is a
negative construction, and your register runs positive.

**EASY WINS** — reads two ways at once, which is the fun of it: wins that are easy, and easy
is what wins. Closest in tone to `EAT MORE`.

My read, offered lightly: **MORE LIFE** rhymes with `EAT MORE` and scales across the three
domains without straining, but `SHAPE HAPPENS` is the funnier and more memorable phrase and
memorability is the whole job of a hub. That is a taste call, not an analysis, and I am not
going to pretend otherwise.
